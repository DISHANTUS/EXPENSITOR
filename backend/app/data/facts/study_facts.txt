1. Spaced repetition — reviewing material at increasing intervals — is one of the most powerful learning techniques ever measured.
2. Testing yourself is a far stronger way to learn than re-reading; the act of recall strengthens memory.
3. Short focused study sessions with breaks beat long marathons, because attention fades after about 25 to 45 minutes.
4. Teaching a concept to someone else exposes the gaps in your own understanding faster than anything else.
5. Sleep is when the brain consolidates what you learned, so an all-nighter often erases the very thing you studied.
6. Studying in a slightly different place each time can actually improve recall by giving memory more retrieval cues.
7. Writing notes by hand leads to better understanding than typing, because it forces you to summarize rather than transcribe.
8. The "Pomodoro" method — 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes of rest — works because it makes starting feel easy.
9. Interleaving different topics in one session improves long-term learning more than studying one topic in a block.
10. Background music with lyrics tends to compete with reading; instrumental or silence usually helps focus more.
11. Explaining a problem out loud, even to no one, helps you notice the step where your reasoning breaks down.
12. The first few minutes of starting are the hardest; momentum, not motivation, carries most productive work.
13. A consistent daily routine reduces the mental energy spent deciding what to do, leaving more for the work itself.
14. Breaking a big task into the smallest possible next step is the most reliable cure for procrastination.
15. Reviewing your notes within 24 hours of learning dramatically slows how fast you forget the material.
16. Hydration and light movement between study blocks measurably improve concentration and mood.
17. Setting a specific time and place for a habit makes it far more likely to actually happen.
18. Deep work — long, uninterrupted focus — produces results that scattered multitasking rarely can.
19. Curiosity-driven learning sticks better than forced memorization, because the brain rewards meaningful questions.
20. Progress feels best when it is visible, so tracking small wins keeps long projects from feeling hopeless.
